ScamGuard AI: A Scam-Detection Assistant

Artifact 1 · Custom GPT + Multi-Model Comparison · Team of 4
Live Demo
Open ScamGuard → View a test conversation
Objective
To design and build a functioning AI-powered scam-awareness chatbot that helps older adults and non-technical users evaluate suspicious messages, links, emails, job offers, and phone calls — applying prompt engineering, multi-model comparison, and the Design Thinking framework to solve both the technical problem of scam detection and the human problem of fear and uncertainty.
Process
Working as a four-person team, we followed the five stages of Design Thinking (Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test). We engineered a structured prompt casting the model as a library help-desk scam advisor, then tested it across three leading LLMs using a realistic USPS phishing scenario. Comparing outputs revealed that detection alone wasn't enough — empathy, honest handling of uncertainty, and a manageable number of action steps mattered just as much. We used those lessons to refine the final prompt, built the ScamGuard custom GPT, and validated it against four scam scenarios (delivery phishing, fake bank alert, scam phone call, and fraudulent job offer), scoring each on six criteria.
Tools & Technologies
ChatGPT (custom GPT builder), Claude and Gemini for comparative testing, prompt engineering, and the Design Thinking framework.
Value
ScamGuard shows how generative AI can be applied responsibly to a real social problem — protecting vulnerable people from fraud — by pairing accurate scam detection with empathetic, uncertainty-aware communication. It demonstrates the ability to move from user research through prototyping to structured evaluation, and to think critically about the ethical limits of AI rather than treating a model's output as ground truth.

Explaining Neural Networks Simply: Communication for Technical Learning

Artifact 3 · Technical Communication
Explainer
Objective
To translate a genuinely complex technical concept, how neural networks learn, into an explanation simple enough for a non-technical audience, while deliberately applying communication and instructional design strategies (analogy, chunking, progressive disclosure) rather than relying on jargon or oversimplification.
Process
Working from Einstein's principle that true understanding shows up as the ability to explain something simply, I collaborated with Claude to build a layered analogy, comparing a neural network to a chain of employees passing progressively refined notes down a line. I deliberately structured the explanation to move from the simplest possible unit (a single pixel) to a complex judgment (a confident classification), applying progressive disclosure and chunking principles directly from this course's leadership communication lesson.
Tools & Technologies
Claude (Anthropic), applied instructional design principles (chunking, progressive disclosure, analogy-based teaching).
Value
This artifact demonstrates the ability to bridge deep technical knowledge and accessible communication, a skill that matters as much as technical execution when working with non-technical stakeholders, clients, or cross-functional teams. It shows I can not only build and analyze AI systems, but explain them clearly enough that anyone can understand what they're actually doing.
The Explanation
Imagine a big team of employees passing a decision down a chain. The first row each looks at one small piece of information, like a single pixel of a photo, and passes along a simple note: "I think I see something round here" or "this part looks dark." The next row doesn't look at the photo at all, only the notes from the row before, and combines them into something smarter: "this looks like it could be an eye." The row after that combines those into an even bigger judgment: "I'm fairly confident this is a cat." That's a neural network: layers of simple decision-makers, where each layer only sees the output of the layer before it. Early layers notice tiny patterns; later layers combine those into bigger, meaningful ideas, until the final layer can make a confident, complex judgment built entirely out of simple pieces stacked on top of each other.

Diagnosing Real-World Data Challenges: An AI Coaching Dialogue

Artifact 4 · Applied Technical Reasoning
Dialogue
Objective
To work through three progressively difficult, real-world machine learning data challenges (missing data, class imbalance, and data/concept drift) in a live, unscripted dialogue with an AI coach, demonstrating the ability to reason through ambiguous, practitioner-level problems rather than recite textbook definitions.
Process
I engaged in a guided conversation covering three scenarios: diagnosing non-random missing survey data in a churn model, correcting a fraud detection model that showed misleadingly high accuracy despite missing 60% of real fraud cases, and diagnosing data versus concept drift in a healthcare readmission model. For each, I proposed an approach, justified my reasoning, and responded to follow-up questions that pushed the analysis deeper, covering statistical validation techniques, calibration methods, and decision frameworks for when to retrain versus recalibrate a production model.
Tools & Technologies
AI-guided Socratic dialogue (SchoolAI), applied statistics and machine learning concepts: MNAR/MCAR analysis, SMOTE, class weighting, precision-recall optimization, Platt scaling, isotonic regression, population stability index.
Value
This artifact demonstrates applied, practitioner-level reasoning under real-time pressure, not just familiarity with concepts, but the judgment to diagnose ambiguous problems, justify tradeoffs, and know which technique fits which situation. It shows the kind of critical thinking that separates someone who has memorized ML terminology from someone who can actually be trusted to make production decisions.
Evidence: Dialogue Excerpt
Coach: Your dataset has 50,000 customer records... the satisfaction survey column is missing for about 35% of customers. How would you handle this missing data?

Me: First, I'd want to understand why the satisfaction survey data is missing before deciding how to handle it, since 35% missing on one column while everything else is under 2% is a big enough gap to suggest it's not random... If missingness is MNAR, the missingness itself is probably predictive of churn, so I wouldn't want to just impute a mean value and lose that signal. Instead, I'd add a binary flag column indicating whether the survey was completed...

Coach: Excellent reasoning. You're thinking exactly like a practitioner... Recognizing that missingness itself can be predictive (MNAR)... Proposing the binary flag approach rather than just imputing away the signal.

[Full dialogue continued through fraud detection class imbalance and healthcare model drift diagnosis, covering SMOTE risks, threshold optimization, and calibration validation.]

Coach (final scenario): Using AUC recovery as the diagnostic for what kind of drift you're dealing with. That's clever, if calibration fixes it, it was data drift. If AUC stays low even after calibration, it's concept drift and retrain is necessary. That's exactly the right decision framework.
